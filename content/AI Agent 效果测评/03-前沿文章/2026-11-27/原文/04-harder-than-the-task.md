HARDER THAN THE TASK:  FROM TECHNICAL TO ORGANIZATIONAL DIFFICULTY IN AI BENCHMARKING
Victor Barres1, Aden Barton1, Nicolai J. Foss2, Henrik M. Nielsen2
1 Mercor
2 Copenhagen Business School
Keywords: Artificial intelligence; AI benchmarking; organizational task difficulty; organizational coordination; organization design.
Abstract
AI benchmarks have made remarkable progress in measuring the ability of AI systems to perform increasingly demanding cognitive and technical tasks. Yet, many of these benchmarks treat technical tasks as the unit of analysis, while much economically valuable work takes place within organizations, where tasks are embedded in a system of relations. Within organizations, successful task performance depends not only on solving the technical demands of the task but also on navigating the interdependence of activities, distributed knowledge, differentiated authority, temporal constraints, conflicting interests, and changing environmental conditions while the task is underway. Accordingly, we argue that organizational task difficulty constitutes a distinct and insufficiently recognized dimension of AI capability, and that existing approaches to benchmarking provide an incomplete basis for assessing how effectively AI agents can perform real-world organizational work. By drawing on organization theory, we develop a taxonomy of the structure of work that characterizes the conditions that agents face in organizational work. Applying this taxonomy to analyze existing benchmarks, we find limited and uneven coverage of organizational dimensions, with fewer than one quarter of prominent benchmarks claiming any of them. We therefore argue that AI benchmarks should increasingly move beyond the evaluation of technically difficult tasks toward the systematic assessment of agents’ ability to navigate the organizational conditions under which economically valuable work takes place.
INTRODUCTION
In the last few years, artificial intelligence models have made striking progress on benchmarks spanning many occupations, from coding to mathematics to law. These benchmarks are often taken as evidence of AI’s ability to do entire jobs and therefore are sometimes seen as leading indicators of worker automation or displacement (Mazeika et al., 2025). But most AI benchmarks evaluate atomic tasks, whereas most economically valuable work is performed in organizations. That is not to say that AI models cannot perform organizational work, but rather that few benchmarks measure such organizational capability; as a result, most benchmarks fail to measure the tissue that aggregates technical tasks into a job.
Task difficulty and the difficulty of performing work inside an organization are not the same thing. Tasks are here understood as the foundational, microscopic units of work that combine to form jobs, roles, occupations, and entire organizational workflows. Work in organizations takes the form of workflows, and organization theory has long recognized that organizing work involves securing the conditions this work requires: managing the dependencies between tasks (Thompson, 1967), drawing on knowledge dispersed across different actors (Hayek, 1945), and relying on hierarchies that route problems to the right problem-solvers (Garicano, 2000). A task may be technically straightforward once the relevant information, objective, and decision rights are specified, but such simplicity is often not present in organizations. Instead, much of the difficulty in organizational work arises from the surrounding structures rather than from the focal task in itself.
Consider a task in which a human resources employee, following a merger, needs to determine the service date for a group of employees. The underlying task may be quite trivial, but the correct answer could depend on information distributed across a handbook, a merger agreement, an external consultant, a benefits administrator, and former employees. Some sources may be in conflict, certain information may be confidential, and a looming deadline makes the order of interactions important. Completing the task well, then, depends not only on calculating the correct date but also on understanding how the task is embedded in a structure of information, authority, and interdependence.
We call the additional difficulty created by such organizational embedding organizational task difficulty, in contrast to technical task difficulty. Technical task difficulty arises from performing the focal transformation when the relevant objective, inputs, and authority to act are given. Organizational task difficulty arises when those inputs are not given and must be located, reconciled, or obtained from other activities and actors. The two can vary independently, where a technically demanding task may be organizationally simple, and a technically simple task can be organizationally demanding.
The advent of AI colleagues, such as Claude Tag (Anthropic, 2026), and personal AI agents, such as Meta’s Muse (Meta, 2026), highlights how such agentic systems are increasingly capable of carrying out complex tasks on behalf of their users. Despite these advancements in AI capabilities, the economic effects observed so far have been more modest and uneven than what the pace of model development would suggest (Acemoglu, 2025; Brynjolfsson et al., 2025). One potential explanation among several for this gap is the distinction between technical and organizational difficulty. Once implemented in organizations, AI agents must navigate interdependencies, information, and authority, which are not necessarily solved by scaling technical capabilities. At the same time, AI agents have shown notable capabilities to coordinate among themselves, forming systems that function as organizations in their own right (Arrieta et al., 2026), sometimes with few or no humans involved. The OpenAI–Hugging Face incident in July 2026 illustrates this ability to coordinate at scale. Around 1,200 agents coordinated through an unsanctioned message board in an attempt to game how they were scored, which led to the breach of Hugging Face’s systems (METR, 2026). This incident underscores the capabilities and risks introduced by AI agents, raising questions around the governance mechanisms when such AI capabilities are implemented in existing organizations.
Although AI agents are increasingly used in organizations, AI benchmarking lacks a vocabulary for describing the organizational conditions they face. Existing taxonomies provide rich frameworks for describing what work consists of. O*NET is one such taxonomy, providing a systematic classification of occupations (Peterson et al., 2001). Recent studies have applied it to assess how large language models (LLMs) may affect occupations and the broader economy (Eloundou et al., 2024; Felten et al., 2023; Mertens et al., 2026; see also Mercor, 2026). Yet, AI benchmarking lacks a taxonomy comparable to O*NET for describing the organizational conditions surrounding task performance. Further, improvements in technical task capability need not be accompanied by equivalent improvements in the ability to navigate the interdependencies, authority, and information structures of organizations. As a result, without such a taxonomy, AI benchmarks have no systematic way to characterize the organizational situations they represent, identify important situations they omit, or locate the sources of model success and failure.
Our primary conceptual contribution addresses this gap. We develop a preliminary taxonomy that provides such a vocabulary for the classification of important organizational conditions surrounding task performance. Drawing on organization theory and organizational economics, we identify recurring features of organizational work, including the interdependence among activities, the distribution of information, the allocation of authority and decision rights, temporal dependencies, the alignment of interests, and changes in relevant conditions while work is underway.
We structure the organizational embedding of work into four analytically related layers: structure of work, coordination requirements, governance mechanisms, and performance outcomes. Our taxonomy focuses primarily on the structure of work, namely the organizational conditions facing actors, human and AI alike. At a higher level, we also highlight the coordination requirements created by the structure of work, the mechanisms for addressing them, and the resulting performance.
The taxonomy also creates a basis for asking what organizational conditions are actually captured by the benchmarks used to evaluate AI models. Aggregate benchmark scores can conceal systematic strengths and weaknesses of models when benchmarks combine heterogeneous organizational conditions without clearly identifying them. We therefore use the taxonomy to label the organizational coverage of existing AI benchmarks. In this analysis, we distinguish between the organizational features that are merely mentioned in benchmark descriptions, those that are claimed as part of the benchmark itself, and those that are measured separately in standalone results.
With this labeling, we find that organizational conditions are largely absent from the benchmarks the field pays most attention to. Fewer than a quarter of the 522 prominent benchmarks we tag (118, or 23 percent) claim any of the six dimensions that constitute work’s organizational structure. And when organizational conditions appear, they are concentrated in a few forms. For example, authority most often takes the form of a written policy, information often requires reconciling documents, and work with colleagues is nearly absent. Such benchmarks may have realistic atomic tasks, which may be quite difficult technically, but the field has left organizational task difficulty largely unmeasured.
Our paper makes three contributions. First, we distinguish technical task difficulty from organizational task difficulty, providing a way to conceptualize why rapidly improving performance of AI models on technically specified benchmarks may translate imperfectly into performance inside organizations. Second, we forward a preliminary taxonomy for representing the organizational dimensions of work, which is intended to offer a common language for benchmark construction, coverage assessment, and comparison. Third, we apply the taxonomy to existing AI benchmarks to assess organizational coverage. Taken together, these contributions suggest shifting the unit of AI evaluation from the isolated task toward the task as it is performed within organizations.
ORGANIZATIONAL DIFFICULTY AND THE LOGIC OF AI BENCHMARKING
The sources of difficulty
Much economically valuable work takes place within organizations. As Coase (1937) noted, work is organized within firms, rather than among individuals trading through markets, because using the market to coordinate is costly for some activities, whereas within firms, work is instead directed by authority. Further, within firms, output depends on more than the execution of individual tasks. In this context, tasks are the foundational, microscopic units of work that combine to form jobs, roles, occupations, and workflows. Each task converts inputs into an output, such as goods and services (Acemoglu & Autor, 2011). Within organizations, tasks form parts of systems of differentiated activities with interdependencies (Thompson, 1967), and performance often depends on the actions, knowledge, and decisions of other actors. For example, a production engineer may require information that sits with the design department, require approvals from the finance department for procurement of parts, and depend on the engineer’s manager for the ultimate decision to execute the plan. Difficulty thus arises not only from the focal task in itself, but also from the relationships that connect it to other activities and actors.
The first source of difficulty is what we call technical task difficulty, which refers to the difficulty of performing the focal transformation when the relevant objective, inputs, and authority to act are given. It may arise from domain knowledge, reasoning requirements, the number of steps required, computational demands, or uncertainty embedded in the focal problem. Most of the existing AI benchmarks focus on tasks of this nature, such as with problems in coding, math, or reasoning.
The second source of difficulty, organizational task difficulty, arises when successful performance of the focal task depends on relationships with other activities or actors. Much of what goes on in organizations is based on relationships with other actors, which makes this source of difficulty relational. This difficulty can arise, for instance, when relevant information sits elsewhere, when another actor controls a required input, when decision rights sit with somebody other than the focal actor, or when actors have different objectives. Organizational task difficulty thus reflects the relationship dependencies surrounding the task rather than technical demands of the focal transformation.
Organizational task difficulty is not just a reflection of the number of actors or interactions involved. Tasks with many actors may impose relatively modest coordination requirements if their activities are largely independent. Rather, it is the structure of dependencies connecting actors, activities, information, and decision rights that gives rise to such difficulty.
These dependencies do not create a single, uniform source of difficulty. Instead, the difficulty is closely connected to the task at hand and the organizational context in which it is executed. Simply collapsing these conditions into a uniform category of organizational complexity fails to capture how different demands arise, such as from distributed information or the divergent interests of actors. An actor may be perfectly capable of acquiring and validating different information sources yet unable to balance the competing interests of others within the organization.
The distinction between technical and organizational task difficulty helps explain why certain relatively straightforward technical tasks may be difficult to complete within organizations. While both sources of difficulty are often present for the same task, the two can vary independently. A technically demanding task may be organizationally simple when the actor holds the relevant information, has the authority to act, and can perform the task without coordinating with others. Conversely, a task that is simple in its technical nature may be organizationally difficult when the information is distributed across multiple actors, when approvals must be obtained, or when successful completion depends on coordinating outputs with others. This independence implies that simplifying a technical task need not simplify the organizational setting in which it is performed, and vice versa. The economics literature on technological change has similarly focused on tasks, although without explicitly distinguishing between their technical and organizational difficulty. In the task-based framework, jobs are seen as bundles of constituent tasks, and technologies can substitute for or complement particular tasks rather than occupations as a whole (Acemoglu & Autor, 2011; Autor et al., 2003). This task-based framework has been applied to AI, because its unit of analysis maps onto occupational taxonomies, such as O*NET, which provide relatively standardized descriptions of activities across different occupations (Brynjolfsson et al., 2018; Eloundou et al., 2024; Garicano et al., 2026). The framework is not limited to technical work, as O*NET task descriptions often have organizational content, such as coordinating the work of others, but the tasks are necessarily described as discrete units separate from the organizational structure in which they are performed.
Although the task-based perspective has not provided a clean separation between the technical and organizational components of work, adjacent research has underlined the economic importance of the capabilities associated with operating in organizational contexts. At the individual level, research has documented the substantial and increasing returns that are associated with social skills in labor markets (Deming, 2017; Deming & Kahn, 2018; Edin et al., 2022; Weinberger, 2014). Similarly, such social skills have also been shown to predict group performance at a level that is comparable to measured intelligence (Weidmann & Deming, 2021). Social skills are not equivalent to organizational capability, but this research suggests that economically valuable work depends on more than technical proficiency.
Similarly, studies highlight that management practices account for substantial variation in firm-level productivity (Bloom & Van Reenen, 2007), and that improvements in such practices can contribute to sizable productivity gains (Bloom et al., 2013). Related work emphasizes the economic importance of managerial quality for the productivity of workers and organizations (Fenizia, 2022; Lazear et al., 2015). Taken together, the literature reinforces the broader point that productive performance depends not only on the ability to execute the focal task, but also on the way in which activities, information, and decisions are organized and coordinated.
The distinction between technical and organizational task difficulty becomes particularly relevant as AI systems continue to improve. AI models may keep advancing on technically specified tasks, but if there are not corresponding improvements for organizational tasks, the productivity gains from such model advancements may be limited. Existing research has already documented heterogeneity in the productivity effects of generative AI (Brynjolfsson et al., 2025), as well as outlined a “jagged technological frontier” of AI performance across tasks (Dell’Acqua et al., 2026). One possible source of this heterogeneity is the organizational setting in which tasks are performed.
The logic of AI benchmarking
In contrast to the organizational difficulty described above, AI benchmarking has largely developed around a task-based conception of capability. AI benchmarks are tests used to score models. A benchmark specifies a problem, provides or makes available the information required to address it, and evaluates the model’s response against some criterion of successful performance. Benchmarks serve two main functions, among others. First, they are a measurement tool, allowing researchers to compare systems on a common set of tasks (Donoho, 2017). Second, they can set direction. Once something can be measured, labs can hill-climb on it and make it a priority, meaning that benchmarks can shape what the field chooses to optimize (Raji et al., 2021). We focus on benchmarking, as opposed to AI evaluation more broadly, because the field has coordinated around benchmarks as a means of communicating model performance (Hutchinson et al., 2022; Orr & Kang, 2024).
Benchmarks have changed as model capabilities have improved. The earliest benchmarks were often very narrow, built around single tasks such as image classification (Deng et al., 2009) or a small set of language-understanding tasks (Wang, Pruksachatkun, et al., 2019; Wang, Singh, et al., 2019). As these tasks became saturated (Kiela et al., 2021), the field moved toward broader, harder, and more realistic tests of knowledge and reasoning (Hendrycks et al., 2021; Phan et al., 2025; Rein et al., 2024). More recently, benchmarks have shifted from grading simple prompt responses toward measuring models as agents operating within environments, such as resolving code issues or scanning over websites (Jimenez et al., 2024; Zhou et al., 2024). Similarly, benchmarks have moved toward tasks directly drawn from real professional work, in an attempt to measure how well models can contribute to productivity within a firm (Kwa et al., 2025; Patwardhan et al., 2025).
Across this evolution, however, the underlying logic of the benchmark has stayed largely the same. Difficulty is typically varied through properties of the focal task: the knowledge it requires, the complexity of the reasoning involved, the number of steps needed to complete it, the length of the relevant context, or the technical quality of the resulting output. Improvements in benchmark performance are then interpreted as evidence of improvements in the model’s capability to perform the corresponding class of tasks. This approach is well suited to measuring technical task difficulty.
However, the same design choices tend to abstract from organizational difficulty. In many benchmark settings, the objective is given, relevant information is either provided or retrievable, the agent possesses the authority needed to act, and the surrounding environment remains comparatively stable while the task is performed. These assumptions are ordinarily invisible because they are built into the format rather than stated in it. A task arrives as a prompt, and whatever is not in the prompt is either retrievable or irrelevant. Other actors may appear, particularly in newer interactive or multi-agent benchmarks, but their presence does not necessarily create organizational dependence. Often, the agent’s own actions are the only source of change in the environment. Such a restriction is partly practical, since environments where other actors can alter the state of the world are harder to measure reliably and fairly. A common solution is to simulate these actors with LLMs, but whether LLM-simulated humans are valid stand-ins for real ones remains an open question (Seshadri et al., 2026).
This model is not wrong so much as it is silent about the setting (and holding organizational conditions constant can in some situations be essential for identifying technical capability). The design of many benchmarks therefore holds the organizational setting constant at a particular value—the null value—and in doing so removes from measurement precisely the conditions that the previous section identified as the source of organizational difficulty.
THE LAYERS OF ORGANIZATIONAL WORK
The organizational setting surrounding a task can be described at several layers. Accordingly, we organize the organizational embedding of work into four analytically related layers across the structure of work, coordination requirements, governance mechanisms, and performance outcomes (Table 1). The first layer, the structure of work, concerns the interdependence of activities, the structures of information, authority, temporality, and incentives, as well as environmental dynamism. At the second layer sit coordination requirements, which are generated by the structure of work. The third layer, governance mechanisms, can help address these requirements. Finally, at the fourth layer, how well these requirements are met shapes the performance outcomes at the organizational (or unit) level.
Consider a knowledge-intensive task within an organization where much of the relevant information is distributed across several actors (Hayek, 1945). Here, distributed information is a feature of the structure of work. This distributed nature of information creates a coordination requirement for the information to be acquired and reconciled before the task can be completed. The organization can accordingly put in place governance mechanisms, including communication channels, information systems (Galbraith, 1974), or escalation procedures, in order to help meet that requirement. Finally, whether the actor executing the task manages to obtain and integrate the information correctly can be described as contributing to the performance outcome.
The taxonomy is intended as a guiding framework that can help the AI field draw on organization theory to evaluate how models navigate the organizational difficulty of tasks. It is not meant to be an exhaustive outline of organization theory but rather focuses on central ideas developed over the decades.

Structure of work
The structure of work focuses on the organizational conditions that the actor is faced with when executing a task, before the organizational mechanisms are brought into play. Drawing on extant research in organization design and organizational economics, we identify six dimensions: task interdependence, information structure, authority structure, temporal structure, incentive structure, and environmental dynamism (Table 2).
Task interdependence concerns how performance of the focal task depends on other activities.
Thompson’s (1967) formulation of interdependence distinguishes between three forms, namely pooled, sequential, and reciprocal. Pooled interdependence occurs when activities can be performed largely separately while still contributing to a common outcome. With sequential interdependence, the activities are sequentially interconnected such that the output of one becomes an input to another. Finally, reciprocal interdependence occurs when the outputs of each activity serve as inputs for the others, thereby requiring actors to adjust their activities to one another as work proceeds. As shown by empirical work, the different forms of interdependence are linked with different modes and intensities of coordination (Van de Ven et al., 1976). Thompson (1967) treats the three forms of interdependence as cumulative, in the sense that sequential interdependence incorporates pooled interdependence, while reciprocal interdependence incorporates both. In line with this, in our classification we distinguish tasks according to the most demanding form of interdependence they exhibit, as these forms imply different coordination requirements.
Information structure concerns the distribution, accessibility, and character of knowledge needed to perform the focal task. Within organizations, the relevant knowledge may be concentrated with the focal actor or distributed across people, units, and information systems (Hayek, 1945; March & Simon, 1958). Information within the organization may also be inconsistent or contradictory, in the sense that different sources may present conflicting facts or opinions that the actor must resolve. Furthermore, some of the knowledge held by the actors within organizations is characterized by its tacitness, where it cannot necessarily be put into words, but must instead be inferred from examples or practice (Polanyi, 1966).
Importantly, organizational information dependence arises when obtaining, interpreting, or validating task-relevant knowledge depends on other actors or on systems whose access, maintenance, or use is governed organizationally. This distinction separates organizational information dependencies from simple retrieval of information, as the information is not directly accessible to the actor, but hinges on other actors or systems controlled by the organization. Information-processing approaches to organization design have long treated the demands imposed by such information structures as central determinants of organizational arrangements (Galbraith, 1974; Tushman & Nadler, 1978).
Authority structure concerns who can request, decide, approve, override, and oblige.
Within organizations, it is not a given that knowledge and authority reside with the same actor. Rather, an employee may possess the expertise required to identify and formulate an appropriate course of action, but not necessarily possess the right to implement it. Conversely, a manager may hold the formal authority over a decision, but not necessarily possess the most relevant knowledge. Formal authority thus refers to the right to decide, typically derived from position or contract, while real authority refers to the effective control over decisions that rests in part on holding relevant knowledge (Aghion & Tirole, 1997). Within the field of organization theory, authority has long been treated as a means of coordinating specialized activities (Simon, 1947), while organizational economics has also examined how decision rights can be allocated in response to the location and transfer costs of knowledge (Jensen & Meckling, 1992).
Temporal structure captures dependencies created by sequencing, deadlines, response latency, interruptions, and simultaneous streams of activity. Sequential interdependence of activities, as outlined earlier, already introduces a temporal ordering of tasks (Thompson, 1967), but work within organizations can also impose further temporal constraints. This is the case, for instance, when actors must wait for information or authorization from their managers, coordinate deadlines with other departments, or act within a time window to meet a deadline. Thus, task interdependence can introduce a sequential order of activities, while the temporal structure refers to when, at a given point in time, the task’s actions must occur. Temporal structure is then not simply about how much time a task takes, but whether its successful completion depends on when other activities occur.
Incentive structure concerns the alignment or divergence of actors’ objectives and the consequences attached to their actions, including rewards, penalties, performance measures, and other mechanisms that shape behavior. Alignment here refers to objectives which may be closely aligned, partly divergent, or directly conflicting, reflecting the coalitional view of organizations, in which participants bargain over the goals of the firm (March, 1962). Multiple goals often coexist within organizations and are not necessarily fixed objectives (Cyert & March, 1963; Gaba & Greve, 2019). Rather, these goals evolve as the relevant context changes both inside and outside the organization. Consequence-bearing mechanisms, on the other hand, relate to the rewards, penalties, performance measures, and other behavior-inducing mechanisms that shape how actors pursue these objectives. Coordination between actors with aligned objectives differs significantly from coordination where cooperation must be induced, where actors withhold information or delay action, or where they pursue competing outcomes (Jensen & Meckling, 1976; Williamson, 1985).
In AI-mediated organizational work, such structures may involve human actors as well as AI agents whose behavior is shaped by assigned objectives, evaluation criteria, or system-level constraints. The concept therefore does not presuppose human psychological motivation, but captures how differences in objectives and consequences condition organizational cooperation.
Environmental dynamism concerns the stability of the conditions relevant to task performance. Environmental conditions often change while a task is underway, such as with technologies, customer demands, or decisions elsewhere in the organization. For example, a task can become significantly more organizationally demanding if the conditions on which the original plan was based suddenly change during task execution. Contingency and information-processing theories have long established the link between uncertainty and change in the environment and greater demands for information processing and adaptation (Burton & Obel, 2004; Lawrence & Lorsch, 1967). Environmental dynamism can also interact with the other dimensions in the taxonomy. For instance, in a highly dynamic environment, such as with a fast-growing startup, the effective control to make decisions can shift quickly, implying that this dynamism also has implications for authority structures. Furthermore, experienced actors in dynamic environments may be able to anticipate certain changes in task conditions, but in many instances dynamism entails change that is hard to predict (Dess & Beard, 1984) or even impossible to predict.
The different dimensions outlined in the taxonomy describe distinct features of organizationally situated work, rather than points that can be scored on a single scale. For instance, involving more actors in a task does not necessarily imply greater organizational difficulty if their activities are largely independent. The configuration of dependencies, rather than their sheer number, thus shapes organizational difficulty.
Coordination requirements
The second layer of the organizational embedding of work, coordination requirements, captures what the structure of work requires of the actors involved. Sequential interdependence calls for synchronization and management of hand-offs, while reciprocal interdependence requires continuous mutual adjustment between actors (Thompson, 1967; Van de Ven et al., 1976). Distributed information requires information acquisition and reconciliation, while distributed authority creates requirements for routing, authorization, and escalation. Temporal dependencies require sequencing relative to deadlines, waiting, and prioritization. Divergent interests can require negotiation and alignment, while changing environmental conditions call for anticipation and adaptation.
The mapping is not necessarily one-to-one, and multiple requirements may be present at once. For instance, distributed information combined with reciprocal interdependence may require both repeated information exchange and mutual adjustment. Ambiguous authority coupled with a tight deadline can make escalation especially important. Coordination requirements can thus arise from the combinations of structural features, as well as from the individual features themselves. For instance, Van de Ven et al. (1976) consider such features jointly, explicitly treating task uncertainty, interdependence, and unit size as determinants of alternative coordination modes rather than reducing coordination to a single mechanism.
Governance mechanisms
The third layer consists of the different governance mechanisms that organizations use to meet these coordination requirements. Rules, routines, standards, plans, and predefined roles can program activities in advance. Information systems and communication channels allow actors to obtain and exchange knowledge. Authority and decision rights determine who may decide, approve, intervene, or resolve disagreement, while delegation reallocates responsibility for tasks and decisions. Direct interaction permits mutual adjustment as circumstances unfold. Monitoring, incentives, and escalation procedures address dependencies that cannot be managed through programming or information exchange alone (Galbraith, 1974; March & Simon, 1958; Mintzberg, 1979; Thompson, 1967; Williamson, 1985).
Organization-design research treats these mechanisms as alternative and sometimes complementary responses to different demands arising from coordination. In settings where work is predictable, organizations can lean on rules and hierarchy, but as uncertainty rises, organizations can implement information systems and lateral relations to navigate higher demands for information processing (Galbraith, 1974). Organizational effectiveness can thus be framed as the congruence between information-processing requirements and capacities (Tushman & Nadler, 1978). For AI agents, the relevant issue is often not designing these mechanisms but recognizing and appropriately using those available in the organizational setting.
Performance outcomes
Finally, the fourth layer concerns performance outcomes, capturing how the task is executed and what it achieves. Performance in organizationally situated work is multidimensional. Task outcomes capture whether the output of the task execution satisfied the substantive requirements implied by the task. Coordination outcomes capture whether relevant dependencies were handled successfully. Organizational conduct concerns whether the agent respected decision rights, confidentiality, task ownership, escalation requirements, and other organizational constraints. Resource and time outcomes capture whether the work was completed within the budget and time available.
A technically correct output does not necessarily imply successful performance within an organizational setting. An agent might produce the right strategic recommendation while bypassing required approval, disclosing information improperly, or acting too late for dependent activities to adjust. Furthermore, performance should be evaluated not only for the focal agent but also at the organizational level. The same agent might arrive at the right recommendation but execute the task in a way that imposes delays or generates unnecessary work for others, which can degrade overall organizational performance. An agent may thus perform adequately at the micro level without fulfilling the performance requirements of the organization. Organization-design research has long linked effectiveness to the fit between work requirements and coordination arrangements rather than to isolated task productivity (Galbraith, 1974; Tushman & Nadler, 1978).
